In 2015, I trained my first MNIST classifier using a Google TensorFlow tutorial. It worked, but I had no idea what I was doing. I wrote about it on LinkedIn with the kind of optimism that only comes from not knowing how far you have to go.

In 2017, I posted that AI would make coding easy. This was before GPT, before Copilot. I also argued that SEO specialists should learn machine learning because Google was already using it to reshape search. These were convictions from proximity — I could feel where things were headed and I wanted to understand what it would all mean.

By 2018, I had built a generative art program that created digital paintings autonomously. In 2020, I earned my CPACC certification in accessibility. In 2022, I launched a 100-day machine learning challenge, using ChatGPT from Day 1 to build study plans and debug code. Then AI tools changed everything.

## The acceleration

Starting in 2023, I began using AI as a thinking partner. I would bring domain knowledge — years of accessibility study, ML experiments, photography practice, philosophy, reading — and work through ideas in long dialogue sessions.

The Memorization-Generalization framework came from this process. I designed a Four Anchor method for quantifying where any ML model sits between memorization and generalization, using degenerate, underfit, overfit, and collapsed models as reference points. The literature grounding (Arpit, Zhang, Carlini, Power et al. on grokking) was mine. The synthesis happened at a speed that would not have been possible working alone with a full-time job.

The same process produced AccessCopilot, a full MVP specification for an AI accessibility agent auditing content against POUR principles. It produced research designs on pragmatic drift in LLM text simplification, adaptive text simplification using Pareto fronts, and a readability benchmark dataset proposal. Each of these built on knowledge I had accumulated over years — the AI accelerated the formalization, not the thinking.

## The numbers

When I finally indexed my own work using a local-first personal memory system I built to make sense of it all, the scale was surprising.

145 academic research documents in iA Writer alone. 558 research-relevant Apple Notes. 163 curriculum notes across 9 Obsidian vaults. 274 Dropbox Paper documents. 6,360 analyzed conversations forming a 24,000-node knowledge graph. Over 1,100 research artifacts spanning a decade of inquiry.

All these years I had one question: *How do machines learn, how do humans make meaning, and can they learn from each other?*

## How generative AI actually helped

Before AI tools, I would have a thought about accessibility and machine learning and it would sit in a note for months, unless something forced me to go deeper. With AI as a thinking partner, that thought becomes a literature review, then an experimental design, then a draft paper — in a single session or two. The domain knowledge is still the bottleneck, but the distance between knowing something and proving you know it got dramatically shorter.

This is what I mean when I say scientific research with AI is possible now. Not that AI does the research. But that an independent researcher with genuine domain knowledge and curiosity, working outside of institutional walls, can produce work at a pace and depth that previously required a lab, a team, and a grant.

## What it takes

It takes ten years of accumulated knowledge that you bring to every conversation. It takes the discipline to treat AI output as a draft, not a conclusion. It takes building your own tools — I built the DATA system specifically because I needed to see my own work clearly. And it takes the honesty to admit that most of the 1,100 artifacts are not publishable. They are thinking. They are practice. They are the substrate that makes the publishable work possible.

The timeline on this site now runs from 2015 to 2026. Eleven years of technical milestones, from a confused MNIST experiment to a 1.6-terabyte personal knowledge system. The AI made it visible to me, and now to anyone who wants to see it.
